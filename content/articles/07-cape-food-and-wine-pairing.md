# Food and Wine Pairing the Cape Way

Most pairing advice assumes you are eating French food. South African cooking is smokier, spicier and sweeter than that, and it needs different bottles.

## Braai

The national cooking method, and the most wine-friendly. Smoke wants savoury, not sweet.

- **Boerewors and lamb chops** → Paarl Shiraz. Pepper meets smoke.
- **Steak** → Simonsberg Cabernet Sauvignon, or a Bordeaux-style blend.
- **Chicken and pork** → wooded Chenin Blanc, or a light chilled Cinsault.
- **Braaied vegetables and halloumi** → dry rosé, served properly cold.

The single biggest braai mistake is a big, sweet, high-alcohol red. It fights the char instead of joining it.

## Bobotie

Sweet, spiced mince with an egg custard top. The fruit and spice need a wine with some sweetness of its own.

→ **Off-dry Chenin Blanc**, or a soft, juicy Pinotage. Avoid tannic reds; the spice makes them taste metallic.

## Cape Malay curry

Aromatic rather than fiery, with cinnamon, cardamom and apricot.

→ **Off-dry Chenin, Gewürztraminer or a fruity rosé.** Alcohol amplifies chilli heat, so stay under 13.5%.

## Snoek and line fish

Smoked or braaied snoek with apricot jam is a Cape institution.

→ **Unwooded Chardonnay or Sauvignon Blanc.** For smoked snoek specifically, a wooded Chenin holds up better.

## Biltong and droëwors

Salty, dense, intensely savoury.

→ **Cabernet Sauvignon** or an oaked Shiraz. Salt softens tannin, which is why biltong flatters big reds.

## Cheese

- Mature cheddar → old-vine Chenin Blanc
- Chèvre → Sauvignon Blanc
- Blue → noble late harvest or a Cape port style

## Koeksisters and malva pudding

Very sweet desserts need a wine sweeter than the plate.

→ **Noble late harvest** or **Muscat d'Alexandrie (Hanepoot)**. A dry wine served with malva pudding will taste sour.

## The one rule that always works

Match weight before you match flavour. A delicate wine disappears under a heavy dish no matter how clever the flavour logic is.
